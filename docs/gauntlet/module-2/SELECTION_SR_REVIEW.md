## accuracy-findings

Per candidate, highest severity first within each.

### Candidate C

- **C-1 (HIGH) — invented league rule stated as real.** L3 Beat 3: "the club receiving the first
  pick is the one the league's draft order would give it to, which depends on on-court record,"
  debriefed as **"the worst team on the floor picks first. The league is built to push stars
  downhill."** The NBA has used a lottery since 1985 and, since the 2019 reform, the three worst
  records hold identical 14% top-pick odds; the worst team frequently does not pick first. Not on
  the anchor sheet in any form. Self-contradicted by C's own L2 SR-15 exhibit (Dallas won the 2025
  lottery and took Cooper Flagg — SR-15, stable). Repair is one line ("the worst teams get the
  best odds, and it's a draw"), but C's stated reason for the deterministic version is to avoid
  trespassing on M4's probability content, so the repair has a design consequence and must be made
  deliberately.
- **C-2 (MODERATE) — the L2 pipe table's proportions do not match the real proportions it claims,
  or C's own ledger.** C states "those proportions are the real ones (SR-2)" for an indicative
  weekly table of NY gate ~$1.1M · in-arena ~$0.33M · local media ~$3.2M · national ~$4.9M. As
  printed, national is ~4.5× the Knicks' gate and ~52% of New York's total, and ~87% of Memphis's
  total. C's own R11 ledger says gate is "roughly a fifth to a quarter of NBA club revenue," and
  SR-1 records the Knicks as the league's **highest gate**. The table is also internally
  inconsistent with C's stated horizon compression (§2: one "week" ≈ one month of season), since
  the NY gate figure is per-night-sized while the national figure is roughly per-week-sized. The
  qualitative payload C wants ("the money you control least pays you most") survives for Memphis
  and is materially overstated for New York. Tuning fix; but C is the candidate that stakes its
  identity on real scale, so the claim is load-bearing and must be re-derived before build.
- **C-3 (LOW-MODERATE) — unverified directional claim.** L1 card N3 asserts national TV pushes
  turnout *down* ("some fans watch it free at home"). Broadcast substitution is real economics and
  the NFL's 1973–2014 blackout rule is its real institutional monument — but neither is on the
  anchor sheet, and in the NBA a national window is usually attached to a marquee game, so the net
  sign for a specific club-night is NOT VERIFIED. Either verify it, or reframe the card so the
  student is discovering a modeled effect rather than being told a fact.
- **C-4 (LOW) — HOOK copy drops a date stamp the ledger requires.** L2's spoken HOOK puts "Lakers
  local media ~$149M, Grizzlies under $10M, same league, same year" on the board; the 2016-17
  leaked-financials stamp lives only in the R11 ledger. The teacher's first sentence of the lesson
  is exactly where the stamp must be visible.
- **C-5 (LOW) — invented indicative constants under real club names.** Capacities are right
  (FedExForum 17,794; MSG ~19,800). Base, sensitivity, per-fan spend and nightly bill are invented
  and correctly labeled "indicative … not final," and C carries the mandatory board line
  ("Modeled on real market differences. Not the Knicks' actual demand."). Accepted as compliant —
  recorded so the label is not lost in build.

### Candidate A

- **A-1 (MODERATE) — invented industry history.** L1 Beat 3: the homestand takes one price, "the
  way tickets were actually sold before 2010 (SR-13)." SR-13 says the Giants pioneered **dynamic**
  pricing in 2009-10; it does not say pre-2010 tickets were single-price. Variable pricing by
  opponent and date long predates 2010 in US sport. The claim is used to justify the round
  structure, so it will be spoken in class. Reframe as "before software re-priced seats daily, a
  club set a price for a game and lived with it," or cut the historical claim.
- **A-2 (LOW-MODERATE) — the M1 bridge is asserted without its numbers.** A closes L3 with
  "Module 1 was the wall. Module 2 is the prices around the wall" and never uses SR-2's cap-growth
  pair ($154.647M, +10%, 2025-26 → $164.961M, +6.7%, 2026-27) that makes the bridge a fact rather
  than a slogan. Not an error; a real claim left unsupported where the support exists and is
  verified.
- **A-3 (LOW) — SR-5 used without its own correction.** A's Packers reveal is revenue-only; the
  anchor's misconception note (record revenue **with an operating loss** — revenue ≠ profit) is not
  in A's R11 ledger or on the board. Of the three, only C carries it.
- **A-4 (LOW) — no stated rights posture.** A never says how real clubs render. B and C both
  commit to typographic wordmarks, no logos, no photography, no likenesses. A's omission is
  probably inheritance rather than intent, but it is the one design that could reach visual work
  without the constraint written down.
- All SR-1/SR-2/SR-3/SR-4/SR-6/SR-11/SR-12 figures A quotes match the anchor sheet, including the
  ~$500M Boston bill, ~$200M+ saved, 14-of-30 / 9-after, Memphis ~$32M, Lakers ~$115M, Warriors
  $833M, Fever 4,066 → 17,036 and six clubs. No invented numbers found.

### Candidate B

- **B-1 (MODERATE) — a real institution altered in a way B's own copy contradicts.** L2 makes the
  national pot "grow with the whole room's DRAW," settling weekly. The real deal (SR-2) is a fixed

- **B-3 (LOW-MODERATE) — an unverified league-economics claim doing emotional work.** SR-6 is

  and, for the WNBA in 2024, revenue sharing did not yet exist (SR-6 dates the first true
  revenue-sharing model to the CBA ratified March 2026). But the claim is not verified on the
  anchor sheet, it is stated absolutely ("kept none"), and it is load-bearing across two lessons.
  Verify and date it, or soften it.
- **B-4 (LOW) — SR-5 without the operating loss**, as with A.
- B's discipline on the two anchors that carry explicit NOT-VERIFIED warnings is the best in the

## staleness-findings

The anchor sheet named which facts age. Checking each design's handling.

**Candidate C — strongest.** Season stamps carried into product copy on every fast-aging figure:
2026-27 tax $200.428M and aprons $209.015M / $221.686M; cap +10% → $154.647M (2025-26) and +6.7% →
$164.961M (2026-27), taught deliberately as a time series exactly as the anchor recommended;
Warriors $833M stamped 2024-25; Packers FY2025 reported July 2026; Fever 2023 → 2024. C is the only
design that absorbs the live-news risk architecturally: its R11 ledger carries "NBA expansion is
live news as of summer 2026," its Kings capstone copy reads "as of summer 2026 … determination by
the end of 2026," and it states that an expansion announcement is a content patch that *upgrades*
L3. Residual: C-4 above (the L2 HOOK's 2016-17 stamp lives in the ledger, not on the board).

**Candidate A — adequate but thin.** $833M is stamped 2024-25; SR-4 is stamped to the 2016-17
leak, in body text, correctly. Unstamped or under-stamped: the $76B deal appears as "about $76
billion over eleven years" with no 2025-26–2035-36 window, so a student cannot tell when the clock
started; the Celtics beat is dated June 2025 but the tax/apron lines it depends on are never
printed, so nothing there can go stale — and nothing there can be checked either. A has no
staleness policy and no equivalent of C's expansion-is-live-news entry; a class in spring 2027 will
find A's real content still correct (its anchors are mostly historical) but its cap/tax context
absent.

**Candidate B — weakest, by omission.** No date stamp on Warriors $833M. No cap/tax/apron figures
anywhere, so no annual-refresh exposure — but also no M1 bridge. SR-4 correctly stamped. B's R11
ledger has no staleness row at all; A's has none either. Because B leans hardest on WNBA content
(SR-6 as "the anchor of the whole lesson") it inherits the steepest-curve risk the anchor sheet
flagged — WNBA cap, expansion fees and attendance records read low within a year — and B has no
mechanism for it.

**All three:** the historical anchors each design leans on (SR-7, SR-8, SR-9 pre-2022, SR-11,
SR-13, and the SR-5 structure) are staleness-safe. C's heavier use of them is a durability
advantage, not just a coverage one.

## rights-source-considerations

Material flags only. No legal conclusions; nothing here justifies retreating to fictional sports.

1. **Marks and likeness posture — A is the only design that does not state one.** B ("teams render
   as typographic names in the Cap Room register — no logos, marks, or photography") and C ("named
   as typographic wordmarks in the product's own visual system. No logos, no photographs, no
   likenesses") both carry the anchor sheet's guidance into the design. A should adopt the same
   line explicitly before any visual work begins; a later visual pass wanting marks or photos is a
   material escalation, not a default.
2. **Modeled numbers under real club names — live in all three, best handled by C, worst by B.**
   C ships a mandatory board line every time a curve appears. A carries it as an R11 ledger entry
   and names it in its own weakness list (§6.6) as a founder-invariant risk rather than a copy nit.
   B extends real names to unspecified additional clubs and describes an invented DRAW rating as
   "published" (B-2) — that is the shape of the problem the anchor sheet asked Economic Truth to
   watch, and it is a sourcing issue as much as an accuracy one.
3. **No design requires a proprietary dataset.** No candidate ingests resale feeds, Sportradar,
   Ticketmaster, or Team Marketing Report tables. A's L1 posts a resale capture line and C's posts
   the Desk-4 resale example; both are model output, and both must be labeled modeled rather than
   presented as market data. Confirmed compliant with the anchor sheet's instruction to model
   rather than ingest.
4. **Leaked financials (SR-4) — all three cite ESPN's published reporting**, none proposes the
   underlying document as a product asset. Compliant.
5. **Named living individuals in a negative frame — C is the only design that gets it exactly
   right.** C's SR-15 copy says "the GM was fired that November," matching the anchor's neutral
   phrasing and not naming Nico Harrison in student-facing copy. A and B do not use SR-15 at all,
   so the issue does not arise for them. C also names the Maloofs only as the selling family in a
   factual sale — acceptable, and worth watching in copy review.
6. **Privacy, not rights, but adjacent and worth escalating with the selection.** A identified a
   real constraint the other two did not: with real market names on `/board`, a market held by
   exactly one pair identifies a seat. A resolves it (≥2 pairs per market, fictional franchise
   handles grouped under the real market name, collapse to fewer markets in a small class). C's L1
   is safe by construction (two markets, 3–7 pairs each) but its **L2 expands to four markets, and
   in a 6-pair class that is 1–2 pairs per market** — the same leak, unaddressed. B inherits it
   wherever "each pair operates its own" real club. Whichever design wins should take A's rule.
7. **No betting-adjacent sourcing appears in any candidate.** Compliant.

## recommendation

### Ranking on real-world strength

1. **C — strongest.** Reality is operated three ways (a real club's building, Boston's books, the
   Kings vote), it is the only design that carries the M1 cap-growth bridge, the Packers'
   revenue-≠-profit asterisk and the stadium-subsidy research finding, and it is the only design
   with a staleness policy written into its ledger and its copy. It also carries the war's most
   severe accuracy error (C-1) and its most checkable magnitude error (C-2) — both repairable, both
   must be fixed before build.
2. **A — strong where it commits, absent where the module gets institutional.** Its real markets
   and its L2 booking verb are the best structural use of reality in the war (below), but L3 — the
   lesson about institutions — runs on an invented rule with a single real exhibit and uses none of
   SR-7 / SR-8 / SR-9, the anchor sheet's institutional spine.
3. **B — real names, invented world.** Every real *situation* in B is watched rather than operated;
   its one real commit-then-reveal is optional; and two of its real institutions are altered (B-1)
   or overstated (B-2, B-3) in ways its own copy contradicts. Its reveal-stage discipline is
   genuinely the best of the three (the Suns' unquoted fee, the SR-4 stamp in body text, "the real
   cases arrive after the vote, never as the answer key"), which is what keeps it from being a
   fandom-free fictional module wearing NBA labels — but only just.

### Commit-then-reveal: evidence or answer key?

All three pass on framing. Every design that reveals a real decision ships a counter-case, states
outcome ≠ decision quality, and forbids a matching score. **C** is the only one that supplies the
decision-maker's *contemporaneous information set* at the moment of the commit (Boston's aging
roster, injured star, ownership sale in progress; both Kings term sheets plus the fact Seattle
offered more money) and the only one that states the honest limit of its own counterfactual on the
board: *"We can show you what the money would have done. We cannot show you what you would have
done."* That sentence is the correct posture for this entire product and should survive whichever
candidate wins. **A** passes with one commit-then-reveal. **B** has none to grade.

### Single best use of reality in the war

**Candidate A, Lesson 2, Beat 2: "Your Building or The Big Room," anchored to SR-6.** The
student's primary verb *is* the decision six real front offices made in 2024 — take the small
building or pay to move the game downtown because of who is visiting — and they discover it was
real only after they have made it. That is the strongest form of the founder invariant: not a real
fact decorating a mechanic, and not a real story revealed after an invented one, but the student
operating the identical decision and then meeting the people who made it. C runs a compressed
one-night version of the same idea (L1's N4 Clark shock, also excellent); B holds SR-6 as its L2
anchor but blurs the crowd-size decision into a section-cost dial, so the real decision and the
operated decision no longer match.

Honorable mention, and the reason C still ranks first: **C's Kings 22-8 capstone** is the single
best *beat* in the war — the class becomes the Board of Governors, votes from two real term sheets
knowing Seattle offered more money, and gets a real 22-8 verdict with a 2026 epilogue that is still
open. C designates it as L3's time cut, which means most classrooms will never see it. If C wins, I
recommend the run examine whether the Celtics HOOK should be cut instead — C itself raises this in
its weakness list and does not resolve it.

### Single most wasted use of reality in the war

**Candidate B relegating SR-8 (the Kings' 22-8 relocation vote) to "optional closing exhibit if
time allows"** — inside an L3 whose entire premise is a room of clubs voting on a league rule that
binds them all. The real board-of-governors vote is the same decision, with real term sheets, a
genuinely two-sided argument, and an unresolved 2026 sequel, and B makes it the first thing a
teacher drops when the clock runs. Runner-up in the same category: **A omitting SR-7 / SR-8 / SR-9
entirely** from a lesson about writing league rules, which also costs it the stadium-subsidy pair —
the anchor sheet's strongest beyond-sports generalization in the module, and the one an 11-year-old
can argue about without knowing a single player.

### Conditions I would attach to any selection

- **C-1 must be repaired before build** (false draft rule; contradicted by C's own Flagg reveal).
- **C-2 must be re-derived before build** (pipe magnitudes inconsistent with C's own gate-share
  ledger row, with SR-1's "Knicks have the league's highest gate," and with C's own stated horizon
  compression). C3's payload depends on getting these ratios right.
- **A-1 must be repaired if A wins** (invented pre-2010 single-price industry history).
- **B-1 and B-2 must be repaired if B wins** (weekly-responsive national pot contradicting B's own
  L1 copy; an invented DRAW rating described as published, and real names extended past the four
  verified markets).
- Whichever design wins should adopt **A's board-privacy rule for real market names** and **C's
  anchor placement table with an operated/reveal stage column** as a standing artifact — the second
  is the single most useful piece of process in the three documents and is what let me audit C
  faster and more completely than the other two.
- **Watch item for the winner, C especially:** C's L2 carries SR-2, SR-4, SR-11, SR-12, SR-15,
  SR-10 and the cap bridge in one 50-minute lesson. Full anchor coverage is not automatically a
  virtue; six reveals in one lesson is how "never disconnected sports trivia" fails from
  abundance rather than absence. If C advances, I would expect Teacher Transfer and Classroom
  review to cut at least two of L2's reveals, and I would not defend them.

**Formal dissent: none recorded.** No candidate's use of reality is disqualifying, and no accuracy
finding here is severe enough to block a candidate from selection. C-1 is severe enough to block a
*build*, not a choice.
