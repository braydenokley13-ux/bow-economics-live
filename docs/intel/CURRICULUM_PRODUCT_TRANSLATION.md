## MODULE 1 — Caps & Payroll (pp. 121–124, 189–192)

**L1 — Salary Cap Basics.** Objective as scripted: the same idea ("a spending limit") behaves totally differently under NBA soft cap, NFL hard cap, and MLB's no-cap-plus-tax — and that choice of rule shapes who wins.
- **(a)** Manage the *same* starter roster under three different cap regimes back to back and watch identical moves become free, expensive, or flatly illegal depending on the regime — never be told the difference, feel it.
- **(b)** build, allocate, trade (constrained), compare
- **(c)** a move that is legal in one regime should visibly grey out / cost double / vanish in another, so the causal chain "rule → legal moves → who can afford whom → competitive shape" is seen, not narrated.
- **(d)** distribution of which regime students judged fairest or most fun, plus disagreement across groups who ended with unequal teams from equal starting budgets.

**L2 — Luxury Tax Basics.** Objective: marginal spending gets more expensive in brackets, and repeat overspending is punished harder than a one-time splurge.
- **(a)** feel a dollar get progressively more expensive as payroll climbs, in real time, not read a bracket table; a second playthrough as a "repeat offender" should make the identical signing cost visibly more.
- **(b)** allocate, price, bid, forecast ("does this signing tip me into the next bracket?")
- **(c)** a running total split into base cost vs. tax cost, with the next bracket line visibly approaching as spending rises — so a student's choice to stop just under the line (or blow through it) is their own decision, seen happening.
- **(d)** how many students voluntarily stopped just under a threshold vs. pushed through, and why — direct fodder for a "was the extra star worth it?" debrief.

**L3 — Cap Jail / Second Apron.** Objective: some thresholds don't cost money, they remove options entirely (no trade exceptions, no sign-and-trade, frozen draft picks) — flexibility itself is scarce.
- **(a)** try to execute a trade *after* crossing the threshold and have it be flatly blocked — the source script's own framing is "cap jail," so the activity should make players feel trapped, not informed.
- **(b)** negotiate (and fail), trade (and get blocked), revise a prior decision now that options are gone
- **(c)** a visible "toolbox" of moves (exception, sign-and-trade, pick flexibility) that locks one icon at a time as spending rises.
- **(d)** compare two rosters with similar win totals — one built staying flexible, one built into cap jail — and have the class predict which is better off "next season" (a natural hook for module continuity).

## MODULE 2 — Money in Motion (pp. 199–220)

**L1 — Tickets, TV & Jerseys.** Objective: player salaries are funded by traceable revenue (game-day, media rights, merch), so a change in fan behavior or a media deal cascades into what a team can pay.
- **(a)** trace an actual dollar (a ticket, a jersey) through the system into payroll capacity, then run it backward: change one revenue input and watch the spending ceiling move.
- **(b)** allocate, forecast, price (ticket/merch price-setting with an attendance tradeoff), build a budget
- **(c)** a live flow diagram (fan spend → team revenue → payroll cap) that updates as students set prices, exposing the tradeoff between overcharging (lost fans) and undercharging (lost revenue).
- **(d)** class-wide spread of chosen ticket prices vs. resulting attendance/profit — an elasticity lesson without the word "elasticity."

**L2 — Small Markets, Big Money.** Objective: revenue sharing lets differently-sized markets compete, and *how much* a league shares is a structural choice with real tradeoffs, not a moral one.
- **(a)** given a huge-market and a tiny-market team with a real revenue gap, dial a revenue-sharing knob and feel the competitive gap narrow — and feel the "big-market complaint" pressure that comes with sharing more.
- **(b)** design a league rule, forecast, defend the choice to classmates
- **(c)** side-by-side spending power for both markets that visibly moves as the sharing dial moves.
- **(d)** where the class converges or splits on "how much sharing is right," anchored to the specific numbers their own choice produced.

**L3 — Why the Cap Exists (synthesis).** Objective: caps/sharing exist to protect competitive balance and league-wide value over time.
- **(a)** run the SAME starting league two ways — one with the rules students built, one without — across several simulated seasons, and watch parity hold or collapse.
- **(b)** rank, defend, revise
- **(c)** a multi-season parity/dominance chart comparing the two rule sets.
- **(d)** a class debate, grounded in their own numbers, on whether the cap should be stricter or looser.

## MODULE 3 — The Numbers Game (pp. 206–225)

**L1 — Moneyball 101.** Objective: under a fixed budget, an undervalued/cheap source of wins beats chasing expensive, obvious talent.
- **(a)** build a team from a full player market where the famous/expensive players are secretly inefficient buys and a few boring ones are the real value — discover this by building and comparing, not by being told the moral upfront.
- **(b)** bid, allocate, build, rank
- **(c)** after building, reveal every team's wins-bought-per-dollar on one shared axis so the "flashy" build and the "value" build are directly comparable.
- **(d)** the spread of final efficiency scores; which few students found the hidden value, and why.

**L2 — Stats vs. Scouts.** Objective: numbers and gut-feel intuition sometimes agree, sometimes conflict — good decisions use both.
- **(a)** face real cases where the stat sheet and the scouting note *disagree*, and make a real signing decision under that disagreement before any outcome is revealed.
- **(b)** rank, defend a signing decision, revise after seeing the outcome
- **(c)** two independent signals (a stat card, a scouting note) shown side by side per player, with the true outcome revealed only after the choice is locked.
- **(d)** how the class split on which signal they trusted, and whether that trust predicted who signed the better player — real disagreement data to unpack.

**L3 — The Metric Game.** Objective: a stat measures one specific thing, and picking the wrong metric to argue value can mislead — including yourself.
- **(a)** as an "agent," argue for the biggest possible contract using whichever real metric best supports the case, then have a classmate "audit" the argument with a metric that undercuts it.
- **(b)** negotiate, defend, rank
- **(c)** multiple metrics shown for the same player producing different "value stories," making metric choice — not just talent — visibly drive the price.
- **(d)** which metric each "agent" picked and whether the class found the argument convincing — a natural rubric for spotting cherry-picked stats.

## MODULE 4 — Draft Day Basics (pp. 221–255)

The source explicitly frames this module as intuition/probability, deliberately deferred from the heavier math in Track 201 (p. 221) — useful precedent: keep Track 101 draft lessons felt, not computed.

**L1 — Why the Draft Isn't a Ranking.** Objective: a pick is a *chance* at a player, not a guarantee; rank-order intuition is wrong.
- **(a)** rank anonymized "mystery prospects" yourself, then have real outcomes reveal a scrambled order (late picks who starred, early picks who busted) — the felt surprise is the lesson.
- **(b)** rank, forecast, revise after reveal
- **(c)** your ranking shown side by side with what actually happened, so the mismatch is unmissable.
- **(d)** class-wide spread of pre-reveal rankings vs. real outcomes — a natural talk about why careful predictions still miss, no probability jargon required.

**L2 — Floor, Ceiling, and the Prospect Pyramid.** Objective: prospects aren't "good or bad," they have a *range* of outcomes, and the right pick depends on your team's risk tolerance.
- **(a)** pick from prospects framed as ranges (not single grades) under a specific team context (rebuilding vs. contending) so the "right" pick changes with situation, not talent alone.
- **(b)** rank, forecast, defend
- **(c)** a floor-to-ceiling bar per prospect instead of a single rating, so risk is visible before the pick.
- **(d)** whether "rebuilding" groups picked differently than "contending" groups from the identical pool — the core teachable contrast.

**L3 — Fit vs. Best Player Available.** Objective: "most talented" and "best fit for my timeline" are both legitimate strategies depending on context.
- **(a)** given a draft board and a specific team situation, justify picking either the top-rated player or a lower-rated-but-better-fit player, then see the outcome "a season later" tied back to the roster built in Module 1 if continuity is used.
- **(b)** rank, defend, trade up/down as an escape valve, revise
- **(c)** a later reveal showing how the BPA pick and the fit pick actually played out for that specific team — reinforcing that the same player is right for one team and wrong for another.
- **(d)** class disagreement on which strategy "won," broken out by team context — no universally correct answer, only context-dependent ones.

## DECISION PACKET — Which "Track 101" is the ground truth?

**DECISION REQUIRED:** This file contains three incompatible drafts of the Track 101 spine (a 3-lesson tiered sketch, a 4×4 mastersheet, and the 4×3 scripted version translated above), plus explicit signs the whole document is an unfinished founder/AI brainstorm, not an approved spec. Should later design agents (1) treat the 4×3 scripted version above as the working baseline to revise, or (2) discard this source entirely and write fresh grade-5–6 objectives from the product bar, using this file only for hooks/flavor?

**WHY IT MATTERS:** Whichever choice is made determines what the next several design sessions spend their time doing — refining existing lesson skeletons vs. inventing new ones from scratch. Building against the wrong baseline wastes a full design cycle.

**EVIDENCE:** Three mutually inconsistent module/lesson structures exist in one file (pp. 9, 11–15, 121–260); the file's own "TO DO" notes call the Track material unfinished ("Finish Track's," p. ~258); no version is marked as approved; audience level throughout skews toward grades 7–9+ rather than the stated 5–6 target.

**OPTION A:** Use the 4×3 scripted version (translated above) as the working baseline — it has real hooks, real sports cases, and is closest to buildable; simplify/re-age it for grades 5–6.

**OPTION B:** Discard the structure entirely; write new grades-5–6 objectives directly from the product bar (scarcity, incentives, uncertainty, etc.), using this file only as a source of sports scenarios and flavor, not lesson order.

**RECOMMENDATION:** Option A, with mandatory re-aging — the module/lesson skeleton and several activity seeds (Moneyball value draft, floor/ceiling prospect pyramid, cap-jail toolbox lockout) are strong and salvageable; the writing level and MCQ/memo mechanics are not.

**CONFIDENCE:** Medium — confidence in the *direction* is high, but no one has validated any version of this content with actual grades 5–6 students or teachers, so real user testing should override this recommendation if it conflicts.

**COST OF BEING WRONG:** Low-medium. Either path is reversible early; the main cost is wasted design hours if a team builds out the full 4×3 skeleton before founder/teacher validation confirms it's the right starting point.
