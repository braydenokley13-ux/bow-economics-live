# Module 2 "Money in Motion" — Design C: Clean-Room First Principles

Senior game/experience design · clean-room exercise · written 2026-08-31.

**Clean-room statement.** This design was produced from exactly two inputs: the Sports Reality
Input (`SR-1`…`SR-15`) and the Economic Learning Contract (`C1`…`C14`, `FL1`…`FL10`,
`R1`…`R16`). No prior Module 2 artifact, prototype, code, or verdict was read. Any resemblance
to existing M2 work is convergence, not inheritance; any contradiction of it is deliberate and
should be resolved on the merits, not on seniority.

**One-line identity.** Module 1 sat the student at the GM's desk, *spending* money under a cap.
Module 2 walks them across the hall to the desk that *makes* it: you and your partner run a real
NBA club's building.

---

## 1. Module thesis and arc choice

### Thesis (three sentences)

Three lessons in which a pair runs the business side of a real NBA club — first pricing a night
whose answer they cannot see before they commit, then discovering that most of what filled their
building was handed to them by another club in the room, then writing the league rule that decides
how much of that gets shared and living a season under their own rule. The journey is forced:
*my money loop → other people are inside my loop → we write the rule that governs the loop*, and
each seam is a door that cannot be opened early, because you cannot feel interdependence before
you have a loop and you cannot design an institution for a problem you have not suffered. The
module ends when a fifth-grader says **"we made a rule, and the rule changed what we wanted to
do"** — a sentence most adults have never earned.

### Arc choice: **Arc A adopted, with four modifications and two rejections**

**Adopted:** Arc A's spine and its forced ordering, entirely. The contract's ordering argument is
the strongest structural claim in either input document and I found nothing to improve in it. It
is also the only arc that differentiates M2 from M1 at the level of *what other students are for*:
M1's other seats were rivals competing for scarce inputs; M2's other seats are co-producers of the
same product. That contrast is the module's finale, not a footnote.

**Rejected — Arc B ("The Ticket Window"),** for the contract's reasons plus one of mine. The
contract is right that its L2/L3 ordering is free (the signature of a topic list) and that C11
repeats M1 L1's allocation verb. My addition: three consecutive pricing lessons fail the
"repeatable core loop" bar in the opposite direction from the usual failure — the loop repeats
*too* exactly, and by L3 the student's hands are doing what they did on day one with a new label.
**But Arc B's best single idea is stolen wholesale:** C12, the loss leader, becomes L1's signature
reveal (modification 2 below). Arc B contributes an ingredient, not a frame.

**Rejected — Arc C ("The Owner's Problem")** as a *frame*, for the contract's reasons plus the
sharper version: Arc C's fantasy is one abstraction step from M1's. "Owner deciding payroll" and
"GM deciding payroll" are the same hands on the same money, and a student cannot be expected to
experience them as different jobs. Arc C also front-loads the money→wins conversion, which is the
single hardest false lesson (FL7) to contain once it is in the model. **But Arc C's structural
gift is transplanted:** the two-scoreboard architecture is present from L1 minute one
(modification 1), and Arc C's L3 is retained verbatim as the *named fallback* if L3's rule-writing
loop fails Stage 0 — exactly as the contract prescribes.

### The four modifications to Arc A

1. **Two scoreboards from L1, named CASH and RENEWALS.** The contract requires this (R4) but does
   not name the second dimension. Naming it matters: "renewals" is the real reason FL1 is false —
   clubs price below the short-run revenue peak to protect the season-ticket base. Calling
   scoreboard two "renewals" makes the antidote to FL1 a real business fact rather than a
   game-design device. RENEWALS also drives next night's demand base, which is how path dependence
   threads L1 without a separate mechanic.

2. **C12 (the loss leader) is L1's signature reveal, and the resale market is its enforcement
   arm.** The contract flags that C12 is a decoration if the ancillary shift is smaller than the
   price grid's step. It is tuned here to move the optimum by ~$9 on a $2 grid — four to five grid
   steps, unmissable. And underpricing is given teeth by the resale reveal ("your $12 seats resold
   for $46 outside the building; somebody made that money, and it wasn't you"), which is what
   discharges R6 without a value judgment: the cheap price is not kind, it is a transfer to
   strangers. This is the direct antidote to FL3, the contract's most likely accidental lesson.

3. **L2's second lever buys DRAW, never wins.** Arc A leaves L2's operated decision underspecified
   ("the schedule links seats"). Watching the schedule is not a loop. The lever is a reinvest dial
   — what share of this week's revenue goes back into the club versus the bank — and its output is
   **Draw**, the number of fans your club's name puts in *someone else's* building. Money buys
   drawing power, which is true; money never buys wins anywhere in this module, which structurally
   removes FL7 rather than debriefing against it.

4. **L3's rule is written on dials, not in a debate.** The contract names its own principal risk:
   a 50-minute lesson that spends 25 minutes voting has no economics in it. The repair is that
   every pair sets a proposed sharing share on their own screen; the projector shows the room's
   distribution anonymously; three 90-second offer rounds with two teacher-selected spoken
   arguments between them; adoption by two-thirds support around the median. Everyone acts, nobody
   spectates, and it fits in eight minutes. The rule then binds two seasons and the room is told —
   before voting — that one club in the room will receive a league-moving rookie next season and
   nobody yet knows which. That is a real veil of ignorance, and it is what makes a big market
   willing to buy insurance.

### Module differentiation from M1 (explicit)

| | Module 1 | Module 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Who am I | The GM | The person who runs the building |
| What I touch | A roster under a cap | A price, a night, a rule |
| Verbs | allocate · revise · bid | **price · host · legislate** |
| Other students are | rivals for scarce inputs | co-producers of one product |
| Scarcity is | a wall (the cap) | a curve (what people will pay) and a pot |
| Uncertainty is | hidden rival bids | hidden demand and other clubs' health |

No lesson in M2 shows a player list, a contract, or a cap sheet. No lesson in M2 asks the student
to judge a player (that is M3), and no lesson uses probability or expected value (that is M4).

## 2. The three lessons

### Scale and money conventions (all three lessons)

Ticket prices and gates run at **real NBA scale** — $10–$120 seats, real building capacities,
gates in the hundreds of thousands to low millions per night. Nothing is scaled down, because at
real scale the arithmetic is already correct and the numbers are already astonishing. What *is*
compressed is the horizon: one night in L1 stands for roughly eight real home dates, and one
"week" in L2/L3 stands for roughly a month of a season. That single compression is the ledgered
simplification (R11) and it is stated on the board.

Real clubs are named as typographic wordmarks in the product's own visual system. No logos, no
photographs, no likenesses (per the rights guidance in the sports input). Student pairs appear on
the board as fictional desk handles ("Desk 4 · Memphis"), never as student names.

## L1 — "FULL HOUSE"

**TITLE.** Full House — *the flow you steer, not the number you guess.*

**PLAY FANTASY (graspable in seconds).**
"You and your partner run the New York Knicks' building. Not the roster — the building. There's a
game tonight. How much is a seat?"

Half the room runs New York (Madison Square Garden, biggest media market in US sports, highest
gate in the league). Half runs Memphis (FedExForum, one of the league's smallest markets). Two
markets, not four, and that is a deliberate trade: with 6–15 pairs, two markets means 3–7 pairs
per market, which is enough points to draw two *real* demand curves on the projector from the
room's own choices. Four markets would make four clouds of two points each and R9 would be
unsatisfiable in a live class.

**PRIMARY VERB.** **Price the night.** (Set a price, and decide what the night is, before you can
know what either is worth.)

**WHAT THE STUDENT ACTUALLY PLAYS FOR 10–30 MINUTES.**

Five game nights, roughly 3–4 minutes each, ~20 minutes of play across two PLAY blocks.

Each night runs the same four beats:

1. **The card goes up** (board + /play, ~30s). Tonight's date, day of week, the visiting club's
   **Draw** printed as a plain number 0–100, and whether the game is on national TV. Every demand
   shifter that will move tonight's crowd is printed here, before anyone touches a dial. Nothing
   moves the crowd that is not on this card (R7). There is no random number generator anywhere in
   the demand model.
2. **The pair commits** (~90s). Two dials on the Chromebook:
   - **PRICE** — $10 to $120, $2 steps.
   - **THE NIGHT** — $0 to $120,000 of tonight's spend on making it an event (halftime act,
     giveaway, dollar dogs). Costs cash tonight; raises **next** night's demand base and tonight's
     renewals. Never affects tonight's gate — the payoff always arrives one night late, and the
     night you pay for it is visibly worse.
   - There is **no revenue preview.** The dials show dollars and nothing else. What the pair has
     instead is their own history — "last Tuesday we charged $44 and 12,100 came" — and tonight's
     card. That is exactly the information a real pricing desk has, and it is what makes this
     reasoning rather than search.
3. **The building opens** (~45s). Board animates the house filling. Each pair sees their own
   turnout, gate, in-arena spend, the building's bill, and the two scoreboards move.
4. **The night's curve** (~45s, board). Every pair in *your market* charged a different price for
   the same card. The board plots them: price on the x-axis, turnout on the y-axis, one labeled
   series per market. The room drew a demand curve without being told what a demand curve is.

**The five cards, and why each exists:**

| # | Card | Why it is there |
|---|---|---|
| N1 | Tuesday · visitor Draw 22 · no TV | Baseline. Establishes that a quiet night is a real night. |
| N2 | Saturday · visitor Draw 51 · local TV | Day-of-week shifter. The same price is now wrong. |
| N3 | Wednesday · visitor Draw 88 (the defending champions) · **national TV** | Big visitor pushes the optimum *up*; national TV pushes turnout *down* (some fans watch it free at home). Two shifters pulling opposite ways — the first night where "just pick the middle" fails visibly. |
| N4 | Saturday · **the shock** · the league's breakout rookie visits | Demand base jumps far past capacity. The right move is to raise price a lot, or to pay to open more of the building — a one-night capacity option. |
| N5 | Tuesday · visitor Draw 22 · no TV — **N1's card, run again** | Same card, different you. The only thing that changed is what four nights of your own choices built. This is L1's path-dependence proof and its cleanest chart. |

**SYSTEM THAT REACTS.** A deterministic demand model, hidden but not random. Turnout is
`min(capacity, base(card, renewals, market) − sensitivity(card, market) × price)`. Every input to
`base` and `sensitivity` is either printed on the card or is the pair's own accumulated renewals.
Indicative constants (Stage-0 tuning job, not final):

- **New York:** capacity 19,800 · base ≈ 34,000 at a neutral card · sensitivity ≈ 300 fans per $1
  · in-arena spend ≈ $18/fan · building bill ≈ $520,000/night.
- **Memphis:** capacity 17,794 · base ≈ 16,000 · sensitivity ≈ 260 · in-arena ≈ $12/fan ·
  building bill ≈ $210,000/night.

With linear demand `q = B − s·p` and per-fan ancillary `a`, the ticket-revenue peak sits at
`B/(2s)` and the **total**-revenue peak at `B/(2s) − a/2`. For New York that is ~$56.70 versus
~$47.70 — a $9 gap on a $2 grid. That gap is the lesson.

**ECONOMIC MECHANISM (mapped to the contract).**

- **C1 (revenue = price × quantity).** Instantiated by commitment before measurement: the price
  is locked before any payoff at that price is knowable, and two different cards have two
  different right answers. *State transition that breaks if C1 is false:* the gate line
  `price × turnout` in the night settlement.
- **C2 (demand shifters).** Mandatory and present on every card. *Breaks if false:* the
  `base` and `sensitivity` multipliers keyed to day, visitor Draw and national TV; delete them and
  every night returns identical numbers.
- **C12 (complements / loss leader).** Instantiated at real magnitude: `ancillary = a × turnout`
  is a term in total revenue independent of price, and it moves the optimum by 4–5 grid steps.
  *Breaks if false:* the in-arena line in settlement, and the second peak on the board.
- **C9 (path dependence).** `renewals` feeds next night's `base`; debt from a missed bill caps the
  next night's spend dial. N5 replays N1's card to make the effect attributable.
- **C10 (invest now, earn later).** The night-spend dial pays out one night later, deterministic,
  with the paying night visibly worse. Not probabilistic — no EV, no M4 trespass.
- **FL1 antidote.** CASH and RENEWALS cannot be summed. The cash-maximizing price strictly lowers
  renewals; the renewals-maximizing price strictly lowers cash. There is no action weakly best on
  both, for any seat, on any card.

**REAL SPORTS CONTENT, AND HOW A NON-FAN SUCCEEDS.**

- **SR-1** — the markets are the Knicks and the Grizzlies. What the non-fan needs is one sentence:
  *New York is huge, Memphis is small.* The visiting club's pull is a **printed number** on the
  card, so a student who has never watched a game reads "Draw 88" and prices exactly as well as a
  fan who recognizes the champions. Fandom adds texture (MSG, Ja Morant, the 2025 champions) and
  adds zero accuracy (R12).
- **SR-6** — N4's shock is the Caitlin Clark demand shock. The reveal is the real line: the Indiana
  Fever drew **4,066 per home game in 2023 and 17,036 in 2024**, the best in the WNBA — and *six
  opposing teams moved Fever games into bigger buildings* (United Center, State Farm Arena, TD
  Garden, American Airlines Center among them). A room that just paid to open the upper bowl finds
  out real clubs did the same thing, for the same reason, two seasons ago.
- **SR-13** — the synthesis close: the San Francisco Giants pioneered dynamic ticket pricing in
  2009–2010, software re-pricing every seat daily on demand signals; variable and dynamic pricing
  are now standard across the NBA. *"The thing you did five times with one dial, real clubs built
  robots to do every hour. Your job exists."*
- **Honesty line on the board (SR-1's open item):** "These demand curves are **modeled on real
  market differences** — they are not the Knicks' actual measured demand. The market sizes are
  real; the curves are ours."

**CONSEQUENCE & CONTINUITY.** Within the lesson: renewals compound, debt constrains. Across the
module: each pair carries **cash**, **renewals**, and their market into L2. L2 opens with a "how
you got here" card that names the specific choice that produced the carried state ("you spent
$110,000 on the night before the champions visited; that is why your base is where it is") — R14's
attributability requirement, discharged as a screen rather than an assurance.

**CLASSROOM INTERDEPENDENCE.** **None, by design, and this should be stated rather than hidden.**
In L1 no pair's decision changes any other pair's economics; what the room shares is *evidence*,
not a mechanism. That is the arc's whole architecture: L1 is the loop, L2 is the discovery that
other people are in it. Manufacturing interdependence in L1 would spend the module's best reveal a
lesson early. (This is a deliberate reading of the CLAUDE-standard "interdependence only where it
materially changes the economics": in L1 it would not, so it is absent.)

**SIGNATURE BOARD MOMENT — "The Two Peaks."**
After N3, the board redraws the room's own Saturday curve for one market and animates the money
line on top of it. Ticket revenue peaks at $56. Then a second line slides in — the same fans'
in-arena spending added — and the total peaks at **$48**. Eight dollars lower. The board holds one
sentence: *"The cheaper ticket made more money."* Immediately after, the resale line, using an
actual desk: *"Desk 4 charged $12 and sold out in ninety seconds. Outside the building, those
seats went for $46. Somebody made that money. It wasn't Desk 4."*

**TEACHER FLOW (for a teacher who has never seen this).**

- **LOBBY (2 min).** Board: "You are not the GM today." Pairs join; markets are assigned by desk
  number, visibly and deterministically, and shown on the board (R8).
- **HOOK (3 min). NOW:** read the card for N1 aloud. **ASK:** "Tuesday. The visiting team has won
  four games. What's a seat worth?" Take three numbers from the room, write them on the board, do
  not evaluate them. **DON'T EXPLAIN YET:** the words *demand*, *revenue*, *elasticity*. None of
  them are said in the first 30 minutes.
- **PLAY block 1 — N1, N2, N3 (11 min).** **WATCH FOR:** a pair that charged the same price three
  nights running — they are your best debrief voice at ADAPT. **TRIGGER:** after N3's curve,
  release the Two Peaks reveal.
- **ADAPT (4 min). ASK, in this order:** "Whose best price was different on Saturday than on
  Tuesday?" → "What on the card made it different?" → "Did anyone charge *less* and make *more*?"
- **PLAY block 2 — N4 (shock), N5 (N1 again) (8 min).** **NOW:** read N4's card slowly; the room
  should feel it. **WATCH FOR:** who raises price on N4 and who opens the building.
- **REVEAL + COUNTERFACTUAL (6 min).** The N1-vs-N5 chart: same card, different room. **ASK:**
  "Same night, same visitor. Why did more people come this time?"
- **SYNTHESIS (7 min).** Named below. **TIME CUT:** if you are past minute 45, drop the N4 capacity
  option discussion and go straight to the N1-vs-N5 chart; it carries the lesson alone.

**SYNTHESIS CHAIN.**

| Link | Content |
|---|---|
| Experienced moment | Charging your Tuesday price on Saturday and watching the building sell out in ninety seconds — or holding it high on Tuesday and staring at 6,000 empty seats. |
| Class evidence | `aggregate.nightCurves[market][card]` — the room's own price→turnout points, one labeled series per market, plus `aggregate.twoPeaks` (ticket peak vs total peak) and `aggregate.repeatCard` (N1 vs N5 at the same price). |
| Real sports | Variable and dynamic pricing (Giants, 2009–2010; now league-standard); the Fever's 4,066 → 17,036 attendance jump, 2023 → 2024, and six clubs moving the game into bigger buildings. |
| Formal term | **Revenue** (price × quantity). **Demand.** **Demand shifter.** **Loss leader** — "the ticket isn't the product." |
| Beyond sports | Flights and hotels that cost more on Friday. Movie tickets cheap on Tuesday and popcorn that isn't. A bake sale in the rain. The store that sells milk at a loss at the back of the shop. |

## L2 — "YOU DON'T PLAY ALONE"

**TITLE.** You Don't Play Alone — *most of what filled your building was somebody else's team.*

**PLAY FANTASY.**
"Same job, bigger world. This room is the league now. Every night you host, the visiting club is a
desk in this room — and every night you travel, you are the visitor filling *their* building. You
still set the price. But you no longer own most of the reason people come."

Markets expand to four (SR-1): New York, Golden State, Oklahoma City, Memphis — big/rich,
big/self-owned-building, small/champion, small/lean. Assignment is deterministic from L1 markets
plus desk number, visible, and never ranked (R8).

**PRIMARY VERB.** **Host the league.** (Price your building *and* decide how much of what you earn
goes back into being worth visiting.)

**WHAT THE STUDENT ACTUALLY PLAYS FOR 10–30 MINUTES.**

Three "weeks," ~6 minutes each, ~19 minutes of play.

Each week:

1. **The schedule posts** (board, ~40s). A deterministic round-robin. Every desk sees: *who visits
   you* (a desk handle, a real club name, and their **Draw** as a number) and *whose building you
   visit*. The room's map lights up with the pairings.
2. **The pair commits** (~2 min). Two dials:
   - **PRICE** for your home night (carried skill from L1, now with a visitor whose pull is
     another pair's doing).
   - **REINVEST** — 0% to 40% of last week's revenue back into the club. Raises your **Draw** next
     week, with diminishing returns toward a ceiling that is the *same for every market* (a big
     market cannot buy dominance — FL7 removed structurally, not rhetorically). Raises your Home
     Base slightly. Costs cash now, pays next week: C10 again, one lesson deeper.
   - Again: no revenue preview (R2).
3. **Both buildings settle** (~1 min). You see your home night *and* what your club did to the
   building you visited. The second number is the new one, and it is the point.
4. **The Handed-To-You bar** (~2 min, board). Every desk's home revenue decomposed. Discussed
   below — this is the lesson.

**Week 2 carries the exogenous beat (R7, announced on the card before commitment):** one club in
the room announces that its best player has left, and its Draw drops hard for the rest of the
lesson. The desks scheduled to host that club are told before they price. The reveal frame is
**SR-11**: LeBron James left Cleveland in 2010 and the Cavaliers' ticket demand and franchise value
cratered — and his July 2014 return sold out season tickets within hours. The line that lands is
not about Cleveland: *it is that thirty other buildings felt it too.*

**SYSTEM THAT REACTS.** The L1 demand model, extended: `base` now includes a substantial visitor
term keyed to the visiting desk's Draw. Calibration target: a collapsed visitor (Draw 15) cuts your
home turnout roughly in half against a marquee visitor (Draw 90) at the same price. The revenue
pipes now settle separately, and they have structurally different shapes — this is C3's
instantiation:

| Pipe | Who sets it | How it moves | Indicative weekly figure |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Gate** | You, tonight | Instantly, with your price | NY ~$1.1M · MEM ~$0.35M |
| **In-arena** | Nobody directly | Scales with **bodies**, not price | NY ~$0.33M · MEM ~$0.13M |
| **Local media** | You, slowly | Grows/decays with your Draw, weeks late | NY ~$3.2M · MEM ~$0.25M |
| **National media** | Nobody in this room | **Fixed. Identical for all four clubs.** | **~$4.9M each** |

Those proportions are the real ones (**SR-2**): the national deal is ~$76B over eleven years
(2025-26 through 2035-36) with Disney/ESPN at ~$2.6B/yr, NBC/Peacock, and Amazon at ~$1.8B/yr —
on the order of $200M+ per club per year before the players' share, which is several times any
single club's gate. The local-media spread is the real one too (**SR-4**): in one leaked league
year the Lakers' local media deal ran about **$149M** against the Grizzlies' **under $10M** — a
15-to-1 gap inside the same league.

**A decision genuinely turns on composition** (C3's instantiation test, which passive display pipes
fail): a pair short on cash must choose between squeezing the **fast** pipe (raise price now, lose
renewals) and feeding the **slow** one (reinvest now, be poorer this week, and let Draw pull local
media up in two weeks). Those are different pipes with different lags, and the right answer depends
on which week it is and how far your Draw is from the ceiling.

**ECONOMIC MECHANISM.**

- **C5 (joint product / externality)** — the lesson's spine, and the contract is right that it is
  the most valuable and least-known thing in the M2 space. *State transition that breaks if C5 is
  false:* the visitor term in `base`. Delete it and every home night returns the identical number
  regardless of who visited. Critically, this **never resolves as "we competed and I won"** — no
  desk's gain is another desk's loss, so it cannot collapse into M1 L3.
- **C4 (market size)** — four visibly different markets, exogenous and unscored. The small-market
  winning path is computable and demonstrated in-session: **Memphis hosting a Draw-90 visitor
  out-earns New York hosting a Draw-15 visitor**, on the room's own numbers, on the board (R8).
  Real anchor: Oklahoma City, one of the league's smallest markets, won the 2025 title.
- **C3 (revenue composition)** — the four pipes above, with a decision turning on their differing
  lags. Payload: *the money you control least is the money that pays you most.*
- **C9/C10 (path dependence, invest-then-earn)** — reinvest pays a week late; L1's carried
  renewals set week 1's base.
- **FL8 antidote, said out loud at debrief:** national money is not free money. It is payment for
  a product the league must deliver, and the league gives up real things for it — start times,
  the schedule, the playoff format. *"Amazon and NBC pay $76 billion, and in exchange they get to
  say when your team plays."*

**REAL SPORTS CONTENT, AND HOW A NON-FAN SUCCEEDS.** Every visitor's pull is a printed number.
Every pipe is a printed dollar figure. The economics is fully decidable from the schedule card and
the pipe table (R12). Additional anchors, all reveal-stage:

- **SR-12** — the Warriors: **$833M** revenue in 2024-25, the NBA's highest and 34% above the
  second-place Knicks; they privately financed the ~$1.4B Chase Center and own it, so they keep the
  concert money and the naming rights and the real estate; the Rakuten jersey patch is ~$45M/yr.
  This is the "there are more pipes than the four on your screen" honesty beat.
- **SR-15**, as the ARGUE exhibit and never as an operated decision: on February 1–2, 2025 the
  Mavericks traded Luka Dončić to the Lakers. Season-ticket cancellations, protests, and the GM was
  fired that November after a 3-8 start. *The price didn't move. The curve did. Who moved it?*
  Complication supplied to the room in the same breath (R15): Dallas then won the 2025 lottery and
  drafted Cooper Flagg. Outcome is not decision quality, in both directions.
- **SR-10**, the designated extension beat if the room is fast: in 2023, amid the regional sports
  network bankruptcy, the Suns' new owner pulled every game off cable and put it on **free**
  over-the-air TV, walking away from RSN fees in the tens of millions. Local ratings roughly
  doubled; streaming grew ~425%; by 2025 an extension reportedly worth $30M+/yr had restored much
  of the money. Most clubs did not follow. Price versus reach, in broadcast form.

**CONSEQUENCE & CONTINUITY.** L2 produces the inequality that L3 exists to argue about — and it is
the *room's own* inequality, not a dealt condition. Carried into L3: cash, Draw, renewals, local
media, and the league's spread. L3 opens with the "how you got here" card, per club.

**CLASSROOM INTERDEPENDENCE.** Genuine and load-bearing. Your home gate is a direct function of
another pair's reinvest decision, transmitted *through the product* rather than through competition
for a prize. Nobody wins by making a classmate worse off; several desks discover they would pay to
make a classmate better off, which is precisely the state of mind L3 requires.

**SIGNATURE BOARD MOMENT — "The Handed-To-You Bar."**
Every desk's home revenue as one stacked bar, sorted by desk number (never by money — R13):
*what my price earned* · *what my own fans brought* · **what the visitor brought**. On most bars
the visitor block is the biggest one, and it is colored to the visiting desk's club. The board
holds one instruction: **"Point at the person who paid for your night."** Then, without comment,
the fourth block slides in on every bar at once — the national check, identical on all of them,
taller than everything else.

**TEACHER FLOW.**

- **LOBBY (2 min).** "How you got here" cards. Board: the four markets, with populations, unranked.
- **HOOK (4 min). NOW:** put up the two real numbers side by side — Lakers local media ~$149M,
  Grizzlies under $10M, same league, same year. **ASK:** "Same league. Same rules. Why?" Take two
  answers. **DON'T EXPLAIN YET:** revenue sharing. Not a word of it today.
- **PLAY weeks 1–2 (12 min).** **WATCH FOR:** the first desk that says out loud "wait, who am I
  hosting?" — that is your C5 voice. **TRIGGER:** the week-2 star-departure card.
- **REVEAL + CONSEQUENCE (8 min).** The Handed-To-You bar, then the pipe table.
- **ADAPT + PLAY week 3 (7 min).** Pairs now know the visitor matters; watch whether anyone changes
  their reinvest because of *whose building they are about to visit.*
- **ARGUE (6 min).** SR-15 (Luka) with the counter-case attached. **ASK:** "The ticket price never
  changed. What changed?"
- **SYNTHESIS (7 min).** **TIME CUT:** if past minute 44, drop week 3 entirely — two weeks is

## L3 — "WRITING THE RULE"

**TITLE.** Writing the Rule — *you designed an incentive; now live under it.*

**PLAY FANTASY.**
"For two lessons you have been running your building. Today you are in the room where the rule gets
written. There are thirty clubs in the real league and there are [N] in this one, and today this
room decides how much of what each club earns at home goes into a pot that everybody splits. Then
you play a season under your own rule."

**PRIMARY VERB.** **Write the rule, then live under it.** (Two verbs on purpose: the contract is
correct that a rule discussed and not run is a debate club.)

**WHAT THE STUDENT ACTUALLY PLAYS FOR 10–30 MINUTES.**

**Beat 1 — The tax that isn't a wall (HOOK, 5 min, a commit-then-reveal micro-round).**
The board shows a real set of books: the Boston Celtics, June 2025, one year removed from the 2024
title, facing a projected combined salary-and-tax bill reported north of $500M under the new
second-apron rules. Each pair locks one of two positions on their own screen — *pay it* or *break
it up* — with Boston's information as it stood at the time (aging roster, an injured star, an
ownership sale in progress). Reveal: in under 24 hours Boston traded Jrue Holiday and Kristaps
Porziņģis, saving a reported ~$200M+ in combined salary and tax. Counter-case in the same breath
(R15): Oklahoma City traded James Harden in 2012 rather than pay the tax, Harden became an MVP
elsewhere and it was judged a disaster for a decade — and OKC won the championship in 2025. No
matching score exists. The board's question: **"The league never banned Boston from spending. It
just charged them. Why did that work better than a ban?"** That question is C7, and the room is
now holding it when they pick up the dial.

**Beat 2 — Writing it (PLAY, 8 min).**
Before anything else, one announcement, on the board (R7): *"Whatever rule you write binds two
seasons. And next season one club in this room gets the first pick and a rookie who moves
buildings. Nobody knows which club yet."* That is a real veil of ignorance, and it is why a big
market will consider buying insurance.

Then three offer rounds, 90 seconds each, on every pair's own screen:
- **SHARE** — a dial, 0% to 60% in 5-point steps: the share of each club's **local** revenue (gate
  + in-arena + local media) that goes into a common pot, split equally.
- **CONDITION** — a switch: must a club reinvest at least 15% in its own product to collect its
  full share? (Real leagues attach conditions; this is the room's only tool against the cost it is
  about to discover.)

Between rounds the board shows the room's proposals as an **anonymous, unsorted histogram** —
no names, no money, no ranking — and the running median. The teacher pulls exactly two voices per
round: "somebody at 10%, why?" and "somebody at 55%, why?" Adoption requires **two-thirds of desks
within ±10 points of the median**. If the room cannot reach two-thirds in three rounds, the status
quo (5%) holds and *that is a legitimate and teachable outcome*, not a failure state.

Why two-thirds and not majority: the contract's headcount-dominance exploit. The pot **is** the big
markets' money, so they must be bought rather than outvoted — which is how real leagues actually
work, and which forces the two sides to trade instead of counting.

**Beat 3 — Living under it (PLAY, 12 min).**
Three more weeks of the L2 loop — price and reinvest — with the pot now skimming and paying every
week. Week 2 is when the rookie lands. The arrival is deterministic: the club receiving the first
pick is the one the league's draft order would give it to, which depends on on-court record — a
variable this module has never tracked and never shows. So it is unknowable to the room in advance,
fully determined in the model, and nameable at debrief: **"the worst team on the floor picks first.
The league is built to push stars downhill."**

What the room is meant to feel here, and what the numbers actually do:
- With **high SHARE**, growing your own local revenue mostly funds other people. The marginal
  return on the reinvest dial falls by exactly the share. Desks notice, and stop trying. That is
  moral hazard, discovered rather than defined.
- With **zero SHARE**, the weakest clubs' Draw decays, and — because of L2 — *everybody's* home
  gate falls with it. The rich desks watch their own revenue drop for reasons they did not cause.
- Consequently, for **every** market including the biggest one in the room, there is an interior
  best share that is greater than zero. Sharing is not charity; it pays the payer, through the
  product. This is the module's intellectual summit and it is arithmetic, not assertion.

**SYSTEM THAT REACTS.** The L2 model plus a pot: `contribution = share × localRevenue`,
`payout = pot / N`, and the condition switch gating full payout on `reinvest ≥ 15%`. The reinvest
dial's marginal return on local revenue is multiplied by `(1 − share)` — that single line is where
FL4's cost lives, and it is one term that can be deleted to prove the concept is a mechanism and
not decoration (R10). Recoverability is protected: the pot never leaves a club unable to meet its
weekly bill at some legal price (R5), and Draw decay is floored.

**ECONOMIC MECHANISM.**

- **C6 (revenue sharing: redistribution *and* moral hazard).** Both halves are required and both
  are computable off session state. Target sentence for the room is not "sharing is fair" — it is
  **"sharing helped, and here is what it cost."**
- **C7 (incentives).** The contract's test is that the same student must act twice under two
  different rules with the rule as the only change, and their own behavior must differ. Satisfied
  exactly: L2's three weeks are the unshared baseline; L3's three weeks are the same student, the
  same dial, the same league, under a rule they wrote. The evidence is their own reinvest numbers.
- **C8 (institutional design / unintended consequences).** The chosen rule is *executed* and lands
  on the chooser's own books, and at least two settings are defensible with different, visible
  winners and losers (low share favors the big markets' bank, high share favors the product and
  starves the incentive; the condition switch trades administrative bite for autonomy).
- **C9 (path dependence).** Closes the module: the league that voted was made by the room's L1 and
  L2 choices, and the board says so with names.
- **C14 (public subsidy).** Explicitly **not a lesson** — the contract is right that it is civics
  in an economics coat. It appears only in synthesis, where it is the best generalization the
  module has.

**REAL SPORTS CONTENT, AND HOW A NON-FAN SUCCEEDS.** The dial is arithmetic; the stories are told
in full on the board with dates, so no prior knowledge is load-bearing (R12).

- **SR-3** — the Celtics hook above; 2024-25 luxury tax: about **$456M paid by ten clubs**, the
  Suns most at ~$152M, and each of the twenty non-tax clubs received a rebate of roughly **$11.4M**.
  2026-27 lines: tax $200.428M, first apron $209.015M, second apron $221.686M. Taught as **prices,
  never as walls** — Boston *chose*.
- **SR-4** — the real answer to the room's own question: in one leaked league year, **14 of 30

  2021-22, ten high-revenue clubs paid **$163.6M** into the pool, with the Warriors and Lakers

- **SR-5** — the cross-league exception that earns itself. The Green Bay Packers are the only
  publicly owned club in the NFL and the only US major-league club that publishes audited books.
  FY2025, reported July 2026: **$453.2M per club in shared national revenue**, up 4.8%; total
  Packers revenue $719M. Green Bay's metro is about **320,000 people** — the smallest market in

**CONSEQUENCE & CONTINUITY.** L3 closes the module. Its carried state is a *statement*, not a save

**CLASSROOM INTERDEPENDENCE.** Maximal, and of two distinct kinds — through the product (L2's
visitor mechanism, still running) and through the institution (the pot, which the room designed).
Distinguishing those two kinds is a genuinely advanced idea that this room will have earned.

Two panels. Left: the pot forming live in week 1 — money visibly leaving four desks, pooling,
and coming back out in equal portions, animated, with no dollar rankings anywhere. Right, after

### Disputes with the contract

**Two, both scoped, neither a refusal.**

1. **R6, scoped to the night card in L2 and L3.** I discharge R6 *holding the visitor fixed*. Across
   visitor states the spread in a seat's outcome will exceed one order of magnitude — a desk hosting
   a collapsed club can lose far more than any pricing error of its own could cost it. I decline to
   compress that, and I defend it on economic grounds: **that spread is C5.** The entire point of
   L2 is that another club's health moves your money more than your own dial does. Flattening it to
   satisfy R6's letter would delete the module's most valuable concept to satisfy a rule written to
   prevent a moral lesson about greed. R6's actual target — that erring high and erring low are
   punished comparably — is fully met within every card.

2. **R1(b), scoped per adopted rule in L3.** If the room adopts a near-zero share, a high reinvest
   may be optimal for nearly every seat; if it adopts a very high share, a low reinvest may be.
   That is not a design defect — **it is C7.** The rule determining what is optimal is the thing
   being taught. I propose R1(b) be evaluated *per rule setting* (no fixed action rule is optimal
   for a majority of seats in every round **under a given share**) rather than over the union of
   all reachable rules, and I would treat a failure of the union form as evidence the incentive
   mechanism is working.

## 4. Uncertainty list — what prose cannot decide

Three loops. Each is a Stage-0 prototype question with a falsifiable threshold, and each has a
named consequence if it fails.

**U1 — Does blind commitment feel like reasoning or like guessing? (L1, the whole lesson)**

and it may have bought unfun blindness: a ten-year-old with no projection may simply spin the dial.
*Question:* across five night cards with history-only feedback, do **≥70% of pairs move their N3
price in the direction indicated by that night's shifters relative to their N2 price**, and do
**≥60% of pairs give an economic reason** ("Saturday, and the champions are visiting") rather than
"we just tried a number" when a facilitator asks mid-play?
*If it fails:* the repair is not to add a preview (that re-breaks R2) but to make the *history*
richer — show the pair the full curve from the previous night, so they are extrapolating a known
shape onto a changed card rather than guessing a level. That is still commitment before
measurement, and it is closer to how a real pricing desk works.

**U2 — Does C5 land as insight or as grievance? (L2, the Handed-To-You bar)**
"My building was empty because of your club" is either the best moment in the track or a fight.
*Question:* after the decomposition reveal, do **≥70% of pairs correctly name the visitor as the
largest driver** of both their biggest and their smallest home night, and does the room's talk in
the following two minutes contain **reasoning about the visiting club's health** rather than
accusation of the classmate? Secondary and equally important: **is the reinvest dial used at all,
or does every desk bank?** If reinvest is dead, C10 and half of C3 are decoration.
*If it fails on tone:* keep the mechanism, move the decomposition from per-desk bars to
per-*matchup* pairs, so the frame is "this game" rather than "your fault." *If reinvest is dead:*
the dial's payoff lag is too long for a three-week horizon and must pay in one week, at the cost of
weakening C10.

**U3 — Can a room write a rule in eight minutes, and does the rule then change behavior? (L3)**
This is the contract's stated principal risk and I have answered it with a mechanism rather than an
assurance, which means it is exactly the thing prose cannot settle.
*Question:* in ≥3 of 4 dry runs with 6–15 pairs, is a two-thirds-supported share adopted **within
eight minutes and three offer rounds**; and does the room's **mean reinvest under the adopted rule
differ from its L2 baseline by ≥2 dial steps** in the direction the share predicts? A third check:
does the proposal histogram produce *convergence through argument* or *herding on the median* — if
the spread collapses in round 1 before any voice is heard, the board surface is doing the deciding
and should be withheld until after round 1.
*If it fails:* fall back exactly as the contract prescribes — Arc C's L3, the league's brake as a
*given* rule the students operate under and then argue about. The Celtics hook, the played season,
and the Kings capstone all survive that fallback intact; only the eight minutes of rule-writing are
replaced. This design is deliberately built so that failure costs one beat, not one lesson.

## 5. Honest weaknesses — where this most likely fails

1. **L2's reinvest dial is one abstraction step from M1's allocation verb.** I have argued it buys
   Draw rather than wins and touches no roster, but if playtest students describe L2 as "paying the
   players," the differentiation claim is dead and the lesson needs a genuinely non-payroll second
   lever. I do not currently have a better one, and that is the weakest joint in the design.

2. **The hands do similar things for two and a half lessons.** The fantasy escalates hard
   (my building → the league → the rule) but the physical act is "set a dial, commit, watch" until
   L3's rule-writing arrives at the very end. The escalation is intellectual; a bored fifth-grader
   in week 3 of L2 will not be consoled by that.

3. **C3 is mostly revealed, not operated.** I attached a real decision to pipe composition (fast
   pipe versus slow pipe under a cash constraint), but it is the thinnest instantiation in the
   module, and it is the one most likely to fail the contract's own INSTANTIATION test under
   scrutiny. If it does, C3 should be struck from the ledger (R10) rather than defended.

4. **L3's shock is determined by a variable the module never shows.** The rookie arrives via draft
   order, which depends on on-court record — real, elegant, and *invisible to the room*. The
   debrief line ("the worst team picks first") is honest and satisfying, but some students will
   experience the arrival as arbitrary in the moment, which is exactly what R7 exists to prevent.
   The mitigation (announcing the event before the vote without naming the recipient) may not be
   enough.

5. **Two markets in L1 may teach FL6 by omission.** Seeing exactly two curves, a student can
   generalize "big city, high price" into destiny. R8's counter-paths are real but they mostly land
   in L2 — which means L1 ends with the wrong impression sitting unchallenged for a day. Widening
   to three markets fixes the impression and breaks the curve resolution; I chose resolution, and
   that trade could be wrong.

6. **L3 has five beats in fifty minutes.** The Kings capstone is the designated cut, which means in
   most real classrooms the module will lose its single best beyond-sports launcher. A version of
   L3 that drops the Celtics hook instead and opens cold on the dial should be prototyped as an
   alternative, even though the hook is what makes C7 land before the vote.

7. **The whole module rests on renewals being felt.** RENEWALS is the FL1 antidote, the L1 path
   dependence engine, and the reason the cash-max price is wrong. It is also an abstraction — a
   number that goes up and down — competing for attention with dollars, which are concrete and
   which students already care about. If renewals reads as a decorative meter, FL1 walks straight
   back in and the module teaches that the point of sports is money.
